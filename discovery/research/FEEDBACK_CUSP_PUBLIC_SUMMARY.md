# What changes when the halo has a cusp?

The promising high-frequency window becomes less selective when the
test includes a cuspy halo and stars filling the central disk. The
frequency spectrum still matters, but the cored example does not
establish that realistic central stars can be spared during core
formation.

In the original cored model, a frequency8 gas cycle deposits much less
energy in the warm stellar tracer than in a tightly bound halo tag.
The directly measured whole-star/tag specific-energy ratio is.00338.
For the fixed inner guiding cohort Rc<.25 it is.08996, compared with
2.31294 for the earlier optimized frequency5 cycle. Most stellar
heating occurs in a small central fraction; dividing by the entire
stellar population makes the apparent cost about27 times smaller.

The separate Hernquist extension uses a stationary halo DF in the
actual gas potential and a centrally filled exponential-guiding warm
stellar population. Both the halo and stellar distribution changed;
this is a new physical test, not a controlled switch of halo density
alone. Its circular stellar frequencies have no finite upper ceiling.
Frequency8 now encounters a real inner resonance, and about89% of the
predicted stellar heating comes from the first radial harmonic.

The weak forecasts for the literal frequency5 and8 cycles give
whole-star/tag specific-energy costs.06393 and.01819. Within Rc<.25,
the costs are2.39575 and.68258. A paired empirical family bootstrap
puts the frequency8/frequency5 cost ratio between.130 and.709; this
does not guarantee coverage of an unseen action tail. Frequency8 also
requires2.56 times the gas kinetic proxy at equal modulation amplitude.
Neither proxy is a measured feedback-work budget.

Independent orbit and phase controls support the sampled weak
calculation. They also expose a numerical trap: an orbit with negligible
weak heating can still have an inaccurate forced endpoint if its central
passage is underresolved. Adaptive integration and finer timesteps
resolve the selected examples, but selected paths cannot replace a
population experiment.

A subsequent independent8192-state experiment resolves finite central
halo heating at the same pulse amplitudes. The energies per tagged mass
are (1.2939+/- .1344)e-5 at5 and (3.8432+/- .4390)e-6 at8. Halving
the timestep changes the means by+.0022% and-.0634%. The coarse8
weighted energy/work screen fails; the finer screen passes. The positive
forward estimator retains capture and ejection across the target boundary
and was checked separately on canonical maps with known energy gains.
The old512-state experiment failed its precision target and is retained
separately. Empirical errors and selected adaptive references do not
bound unseen rare paths.

The finite halo result is small: about15.0 and4.45 parts per million
of the tag binding energy. It does not establish a core. The stellar
values above remain weak forecasts, so a finite-amplitude cuspy stellar/
halo heating ratio is still untested. Same-state finite-minus-weak halo
differences are only1.75 and1.99 empirical standard errors; an ensemble
nonlinear correction is unresolved.

The gas stays positive and has finite support in both models. The
experiments track common-potential work and retain negative signed
responses. Their tightly bound halo tag is not all mass inside the
chosen core radius. The tested energy deposit is small, and no
self-consistent cusp-to-core transformation, observational stellar
survival bound or vertical-survival result follows.

[Feedback-driven heating](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.0499),
[resonant core formation](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5412), and
[stellar kinematic consequences](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.01235)
are established research subjects. The contribution being tested is
a quantitative selectivity window or a lower cost bound for an
explicitly specified population and driving family.
